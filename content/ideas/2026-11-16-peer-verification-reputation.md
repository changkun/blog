**Context**

Your note is about a tension in software and knowledge work right now. Everyone can see that agents generate code that is "incredibly good and working," yet well-run organizations stay very careful about details. They use **peer-to-peer pressure to verify output and keep slop out**. Your point is that this verification does two jobs. It filters quality, and it also builds an **internal profile** of each person that they can't fake. To earn more trust from peers, and so the right to push things forward, a person has to show real understanding. If they don't, a **bad profile forms in people's minds and damages their reputation**. Your proposed survival skill is the ability to **work on several things at once and still go into detail, while thinking at a higher level of abstraction**. That's how a person stays useful as AI becomes able to exceed human abilities in many areas.

This touches software engineering (code review), organizational behavior (trust and reputation), signaling theory and human–AI teaming. It matters now because generating output has become nearly free, so the costly step has moved to *verification* and *attribution of understanding*.

**Key Insights**

1. **Agents can write impressive code, but that isn't the same as code that ships in a real codebase.** METR ran a randomized trial with 16 experienced open-source developers on 246 real issues. With early-2025 AI tools they took **19% longer**, yet they believed AI had sped them up by 20%. METR suggests AI may do relatively worse "in settings with very high quality standards, or with many implicit requirements," which are exactly the details that careful organizations enforce ([METR, 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)). METR marks these results as outdated and has published a February 2026 update, which I couldn't fetch to check. The more lasting lesson is the gap between how fast people *feel* and how fast they *are*. That gap is why outside peer checks matter: people judge their own AI-assisted work badly.

2. **Slop is now an organizational cost.** Niederhoffer, Hancock et al. call it "workslop" in HBR. They link it to a paradox: AI use at work has doubled since 2023, while an MIT Media Lab report found **95% of organizations see no measurable return** ([HBR, 2025](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity)). As I recall, the article also reports that people who receive workslop rate the sender as less capable and reliable. That would be direct evidence for your "bad profile" mechanism, but the fetched text was cut off before that section, so treat it as unconfirmed.

3. **Peer verification only works when identity persists.** Curl's maintainer Daniel Stenberg reports that about **20% of 2025 security submissions were AI slop** and only about 5% were genuine. Each report ties up 3–4 of the seven team members for 30 minutes to 3 hours. He also says plainly that **"reputation doesn't help"** on HackerOne, because new users "can just create a new account next week" ([Stenberg, "Death by a thousand slops"](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2025/07/14/death-by-a-thousand-slops/)). This sharpens your claim. A profile "cannot be faked" only inside a group where people stick around and know each other, like a team or a long-running set of reviewers. Open, anonymous systems lack this, which is why commenters there suggested web-of-trust vouching, refundable fees, or screen recordings of a working reproduction. All of these add cost back in to stand in for a reputation the system can't hold.

4. **Code review was always partly about reputation and shared knowledge.** Sadowski, Bacchelli et al. studied 9 million reviewed changes at Google. They found review motivations go beyond catching defects to include knowledge transfer and accountability ([Modern Code Review: A Case Study at Google, ICSE SEIP 2018](https://research.google/pubs/modern-code-review-a-case-study-at-google/)). Bacchelli & Bird's earlier Microsoft study (ICSE 2013) found the same: the main outcome is *understanding*, not just bug-finding. So AI doesn't create the need for peer checks. It removes the old proxy, "they wrote it, so they understand it," and leaves the check for understanding as the main thing review does.

5. **Why the profile is hard to fake: costly signals.** Spence's job-market signaling (1973) and Zahavi's handicap principle apply here. A signal is believable when it's expensive to produce without the real quality behind it. Polished code or prose used to be such a signal, and AI has made it cheap. Live behavior is still expensive: answering follow-up questions, defending trade-offs, spotting subtle edge cases. In Mayer, Davis & Schoorman's trust model (1995), trust rests on *ability*, *benevolence* and *integrity*. Peer review mostly tests ability, but passing slop off as your own work also costs you on integrity.

6. **Your multi-level abstraction skill matches observed changes in how people use AI.** Lee, Sarkar et al. surveyed 319 knowledge workers (CHI 2025). They found GenAI shifts critical thinking toward **"information verification, response integration, and task stewardship."** **Higher confidence in GenAI predicted less critical thinking**, while higher self-confidence predicted more ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)). Being able to "go into detail while mentally working on a higher level abstraction" is stewardship. It also requires keeping enough self-confidence, grounded in real expertise, to keep checking.

7. **Counterpoint: the multitasking claim needs care.** Research on task switching (Monsell, 2003) and on "attention residue" (Leroy, 2009) shows real costs when people split attention. The skill you describe probably isn't true parallel attention. It's more likely fast, well-timed zooming between levels: knowing *when* a detail deserves a deep look. That makes it a judgment skill, not a bandwidth skill, and judgment is exactly what peer review tests.

8. **A possible failure mode: reputation systems can be gamed or become cliquey.** Trust profiles can harden into in-groups that shut out newcomers, a worry raised in the curl comments. They also reward *looking* like you understand. As AI gets better at producing convincing explanations, even live answers in review could be scripted. That would push verification toward higher-friction formats: in-person discussion, reproduction, and owning outcomes over time.

**Open Questions**

- If understanding becomes the scarce thing being verified, will organizations formalize the "internal profile," for example as tracked records of who reliably catches what in review? And would making it explicit destroy the property that makes it hard to fake (Goodhart's law)?
- When agents become reliable reviewers themselves, does human peer pressure keep its function, or does trust move from "this person understands" to "this person knows which checks to run and when to escalate"? In other words, does reputation shift from knowing the content to judging how verification should be done?
